So, you're not going to lose money on an unfulfilled order like with some outright fraudulent sites. However, the catch lies in their business model and how they achieve those incredibly low prices, which often involves significant **privacy concerns** and a less-than-stellar track record on data handling. The real question isn't whether it's a scam, but whether you're comfortable with the **trade-offs** involved. Temu's aggressive data collection practices are well-documented. They gather a lot more personal information than many other shopping apps, including details about your device, network, and even other apps installed on your phone. ## What Does Temu Actually Do With Your Data?

Temu collects a wide range of your personal information, browsing habits, and purchase history, which it uses primarily for targeted advertising and to improve its services, though concerns exist about potential broader data sharing.

Temu, like most large online retailers, is quite upfront in its privacy policy about the data it collects. When you sign up, you're handing over your name, email address, phone number, and shipping details. As you use the app, it tracks your browsing habits – what you click on, what you search for, and what you add to your cart. Naturally, it also logs your full purchase history, including payment information (though it typically uses third-party processors for the actual transactions). All of this is standard practice for e-commerce, designed to give you a personalized shopping experience. They state that this data is used to process your orders, provide customer support, and, most importantly, for \*\*targeted advertising\*\*. This means showing you products you're more likely to buy based on your past activity. They also use it for internal analytics to understand market trends and improve their app's functionality. This is where personal information and your browsing habits become valuable — it helps them optimize everything from product recommendations to the layout of their website. Now, where things get a bit more opaque is with \*\*third parties\*\*. Temu's privacy policy mentions sharing data with service providers, business partners, and affiliates. While they claim this is for purposes like payment processing, shipping, and marketing, the specifics of who these "partners" are and the full extent of data shared aren't always crystal clear. It's not uncommon for apps from certain regions to have broader data-sharing practices than what you might expect from a Western company, and this has led to some public scrutiny regarding Temu's operations. The biggest concern often boils down to trust. While Temu asserts that they don't sell your data directly, the line between "sharing with partners for marketing" and "selling for profit" can sometimes feel blurry to users. For a privacy-first company like OllaVPN, this distinction is crucial. When you're using an app that collects this much data, even if it's for legitimate business purposes, it's always wise to be aware of what you're sharing and consider how you might protect your broader online activities.

## What Are the Genuine Privacy Concerns About Temu?

Temu presents genuine privacy concerns through its aggressive data collection, potential for [data breaches](/blog/what-is-a-data-breach/), sharing with its parent company, and broad app permissions.

You're right to be wary. While many apps collect data, Temu stands out because of the sheer breadth and depth of information it seeks, often going beyond what seems necessary for an e-commerce platform. It's not just about what you buy; it's about what else they can learn about you. The primary concern revolves around its [**excessive permissions**](/blog/what-are-excessive-app-permissions/) on mobile devices, requesting access to things like your contacts, microphone, camera, and even Bluetooth and Wi-Fi network information. While some of these might have a tenuous link to app functionality (like using the camera for image search), many don't, raising red flags about data exploitation.

Another significant worry is the potential for [**data breaches**](/blog/how-does-a-vpn-prevent-data-breaches/). The more data a company collects, the larger the target it becomes for cybercriminals. If Temu's security measures aren't absolutely top-notch, all that personal and [**financial data risk**](/blog/securing-your-financial-data-online/) – your payment information, shipping addresses, phone numbers, and browsing habits – could end up in the wrong hands. We've seen this happen with countless companies, and Temu's rapid growth and global reach only amplify this risk.

Then there's the issue of [**data sharing with its parent company**](/blog/data-sharing-with-parent-company-risks/), PDD Holdings. PDD Holdings also owns Pinduoduo, an app that was suspended from Google Play due to malware concerns. While Temu claims to operate independently, the underlying ownership structure and the history of its sister app fuel [**surveillance concerns**](/blog/surveillance-concerns-with-apps/). It's not always clear how data flows between these entities and what protections are in place. This lack of transparency makes it difficult to trust that your data isn't being used in ways you wouldn't approve of, or worse, shared with state-backed actors.

Ultimately, while convenience and low prices are appealing, you have to weigh that against the potential privacy cost. For many, the extensive data collection, broad permissions, and the track record of its parent company are enough reason to approach Temu with extreme caution, or avoid it altogether. Using a VPN can help shield your browsing activity from your ISP, but it won't stop the app itself from collecting data once it's on your device.

## What Red Flags Mean You Should Walk Away From Temu?

You should walk away from Temu if you encounter unusual app permissions, unsolicited communications, suspicious payment requests, a lack of transparency, or consistently poor customer service.

Temu has exploded in popularity, but like with any online platform, it's wise to be cautious. If you notice the app asking for \*\*unusual app permissions\*\* that seem unrelated to its function—like access to your microphone, camera, or contacts when you're just trying to buy a T-shirt—that's a major red flag. Most e-commerce apps don't need that level of access to your device, and granting it could give them more data than you're comfortable sharing. Always review permissions carefully before installing or updating, and if something feels off, it probably is. Another warning sign is if you start receiving \*\*unsolicited communications\*\* that feel spammy or targeted after using the platform. This could be emails, texts, or even calls that you didn't sign up for, which might indicate your data is being used in ways you didn't authorize. Similarly, be extremely wary of \*\*suspicious payment requests\*\*. Temu should only process payments through its official channels. If you get an email or message asking you to pay outside the app or website, or to click a link to "verify" your payment information, assume it's a phishing attempt. Legitimate companies don't typically ask for payment details this way. A general \*\*lack of transparency\*\* about how they handle user data, where products come from, or their business practices should also make you pause. While many platforms operate with some level of opacity, an excessive lack of clear information when you're trying to understand their policies is concerning. Finally, if you consistently experience \*\*poor customer service\*\*—unanswered queries, unhelpful responses, or difficulty resolving issues—it suggests that if something goes wrong with an order, you might be left without support. While a single bad experience can happen anywhere, a pattern of neglect is a sign to consider alternatives, especially when dealing with financial transactions and personal data.

## What Permissions Should I Actually Grant the Temu App?

You should grant the Temu app the absolute minimum permissions it needs to function, denying everything else by default.

When you install an app like Temu, it often asks for a slew of permissions up front. Your best bet for privacy is to deny almost all of them initially. Temu primarily needs network access to show you products and process orders. Anything beyond that should be treated with suspicion until you specifically need a feature that requires it. For instance, you don't need to give it access to your **microphone access** or **camera access** just to browse products. Think about what you're actually doing in the app. Are you making a video call? No. Are you taking a picture to upload for a return? Maybe, but you can grant that permission \*when\* you need it, and then revoke it afterwards. Permissions like **contacts** or precise **location services** are almost never necessary for an e-commerce app, and granting them just gives the app more data about you that it doesn't truly need to operate. Even **storage access** might not be strictly necessary for basic shopping, though some apps use it to cache images or download invoices. Again, if you encounter a specific function that fails without it, grant it temporarily. The general rule is: deny by default, and only grant a permission if a core feature you want to use absolutely requires it, and even then, consider if you can revoke it later. Your phone's operating system likely allows you to manage these permissions at any time through its settings.

## How we evaluate "is X safe" questions

Safety questions are deceptively hard because "safe" depends entirely on what you're trying to be safe from. The framework we use whenever we publish a safety evaluation:

**Identify the specific threat model.** "Is X safe" without a threat model is meaningless. Safe from getting your credit card stolen? Safe from your purchase being tracked in advertising profiles? Safe from your data being shared with foreign governments? Each is a different question with a different answer. We name the threats explicitly before evaluating against them.

**Distinguish documented behavior from hypothetical risk.** Many "is X safe" articles read like indictments because they list every theoretical risk without weighting how likely each is. We separate documented incidents (a thing that has actually happened, with date and source) from theoretical risks (a thing that could happen but hasn't been observed). Both matter; treating them identically misleads readers.

**Compare to alternatives, not to perfection.** Nothing on the internet is perfectly safe. The honest question is "is X safer or less safe than the realistic alternatives." Sometimes the alternative is "not doing the thing at all" (which is fine when the thing is optional); sometimes the alternative is "doing the same thing with a different provider" (which requires comparing across providers).

**State what would change the answer.** A safety evaluation should include the conditions under which it would change. If new evidence emerges, if the operator's ownership changes, if a third-party audit is published, those events should update the conclusion. We say what we'd watch for.
