## What Should You Expect From Any Honest Free VPN?

You should expect a free VPN to have some limitations, but never at the expense of your privacy or security.

An honest free VPN makes a clear trade-off: you get solid privacy and security, but with certain caps on performance or features. The most common and transparent limitation is a **speed cap**. That's how OllaVPN's free tier works, giving you 10 Mbps – enough for most daily tasks – without ever touching your personal data or showing you ads. You should also expect no **data cap**; if a free VPN limits your data, it's often a ploy to get you to upgrade quickly, or they're funding themselves in other, less transparent ways. An honest free VPN lets you use it as much as you need, even if it's at a reduced speed. You should also expect full access to their **server network**, even if it's just one server in every country they support. Some "free" VPNs restrict you to only a few locations, which can limit their usefulness for bypassing geo-restrictions or finding a less congested server. OllaVPN gives you access to every country in our network on the free plan, so you're not locked out of options. Finally, an honest free VPN shouldn't skimp on **basic security features**. That means strong encryption (like WireGuard), a kill switch to prevent accidental data leaks, and a strict no-logs policy. These are non-negotiables. If a free VPN is advertising "unlimited data, unlimited speed, all servers!" without a clear business model, you should be very, very suspicious. They're likely making money from your data, which defeats the entire purpose of using a VPN.

## What Are Common Mistakes People Make When Picking a Free VPN?

The most common mistake people make when picking a free VPN is assuming "free" means "private" or "secure," when often it means your data is being exploited.

It's easy to fall for the promise of a free service, especially when it comes to something as important as your online privacy. However, many ad-funded free VPNs need to make money somehow, and if you're not paying with cash, you're usually paying with something else. This often leads to concerning practices like \*\*data selling\*\*, where your browsing habits and personal information are collected and sold to advertisers or other third parties without your explicit consent. You think you're protecting your privacy, but you're actually handing it over to a different set of hands. Another big red flag is \*\*ad injection\*\*. Some free VPNs modify the websites you visit or the apps you use to insert their own advertisements, which can be annoying at best and malicious at worst. This isn't just about seeing more ads; it can introduce security vulnerabilities or even lead to \*\*malware\*\* being installed on your device. Beyond that, many free services skimp on security fundamentals, leading to \*\*poor encryption\*\* or outdated protocols that don't actually protect your data from savvy snoopers. Then there are the \*\*logging practices\*\*. A VPN's core promise is to hide your online activity, but if a free VPN keeps detailed logs of what you do online, that promise is broken. These logs can be subpoenaed by authorities or hacked, exposing everything you thought was private. Always look for a service that explicitly states a no-logs policy. At OllaVPN, we fund our free tier through our paid Plus subscriptions, so we don't have to resort to any of these tactics. Our free users get the same no-logs, no-ads, no-data-selling guarantee as our paid users, just with a speed cap.

## How to evaluate any "answer" you read on the internet

You'll find competing answers to the same question across many sites. A few signals that distinguish trustworthy answers from junk:

**Cites primary sources where they exist.** Government law, NIST publications, IETF RFCs, court rulings, academic papers — these are primary sources. A guide that cites them is generally more reliable than one that doesn't.

**Names an author.** Anonymous "by editorial team" content can be solid, but a named author with verifiable credentials is more accountable. Author bylines on YMYL (your-money-or-your-life) topics like privacy and security are a meaningful trust signal.

**Date-stamped recently.** Outdated answers are worse than no answer because they're confidently wrong. Look for an explicit "last updated" date, not just a publication date.

**Discloses conflicts of interest.** Most privacy-and-networking content is published by operators who have a commercial interest in the conclusion. Honest content names the conflict explicitly. Hidden conflicts are the bigger red flag.

**Distinguishes between certainty and uncertainty.** Hedged language ("typically," "depends on," "in most cases") is often more accurate than confident absolutes. The author who claims certainty about a question that genuinely has nuance is probably oversimplifying.
