## What does "really free" actually mean for a VPN in India? [#](#what-does-really-free-actually-mean-for-a-vpn-in-india)

**Quick Answer**

"Really free" means you can download, install, and use the VPN indefinitely without ever providing payment details or being auto-enrolled in a paid subscription  -  and "no logs" means the provider doesn't retain records of your browsing activity, connection timestamps, or IP address in a way that could identify you later.

These phrases are used loosely enough in app listings aimed at Indian users that they're worth pinning down before comparing any actual products.

"No credit card" should mean exactly that  -  no card on file at all, not a card charged ₹0 today that silently begins billing after a short trial window. This pattern shows up constantly in VPN listings on the Play Store and App Store: a "free" download that actually starts a subscription through the platform's in-app purchase system, with billing kicking in automatically after 3 or 7 days unless you remember to cancel. That's a trial with an auto-renewal trap wearing a "free" label, not a free VPN.

"No logs" should mean the provider doesn't store data that could reconstruct your browsing activity  -  no record of which sites you visited, no timestamped connection logs tied to your identity, no retained traffic history. This phrase gets stretched further than almost any other in VPN marketing, because a provider can technically avoid logging your browsing history while still logging connection timestamps, device identifiers, or approximate location  -  details that, combined, can still identify you. A meaningful no-logs claim should specify exactly what categories of data aren't retained, ideally backed by an independent security audit.

Put together, "really free, no credit card, no logs" is a genuinely high bar  -  free at the point of use, and free of the surveillance business model that funds a lot of "free" software. Most listings that use these exact words in India's app stores don't actually clear it.

## Why does India specifically need careful VPN vetting? [#](#why-does-india-specifically-need-careful-vpn-vetting)

**Quick Answer**

India has one of the largest and most crowded "free VPN" app markets in the world, a regulatory environment ([CERT-In](/blog/best-free-vpn-for-india.html)'s 2022 directions) that shapes what some providers log or don't operate in-country, and widely varying mobile network conditions that make an honest speed cap more noticeable than in some other markets  -  all of which make careful vetting more important, not less.

A few things about the Indian VPN landscape specifically are worth understanding before you pick anything.

The sheer number of "free VPN" apps aimed at Indian users is unusually large. India is one of the largest mobile-app markets in the world, and VPN apps consistently rank among the most-downloaded utility categories on both Play Store and App Store. That scale attracts serious operators, but it also attracts a large number of low-effort apps whose entire business model is ad impressions or data resale, published specifically to capture search volume around terms like "free VPN India" or "unlimited VPN."

CERT-In's 2022 data-retention directions changed the landscape for VPN operators serving India. India's Computer Emergency Response Team issued directions requiring VPN providers with servers physically located in India to retain customer logs  -  including names, IP addresses, and usage patterns  -  for a minimum period. In response, several well-known privacy-focused VPN providers withdrew their physical servers from India entirely, instead offering "virtual" India-location servers hosted elsewhere that still present an Indian IP address. This matters directly for the "no logs" question: a provider's actual server location, and whether that location falls under CERT-In's directions, is a genuinely relevant fact to check, not a technicality.

Network conditions in India vary widely by region and carrier, which makes an honest, clearly stated speed cap more useful information than in markets with more uniformly fast broadband  -  you're better positioned to judge whether a 10 Mbps cap will be noticeable on your specific connection if the provider states the number plainly rather than hiding behind vague language like "blazing fast."

None of this means every VPN operating in India is compromised  -  it means the vetting checklist above matters more here than it might in a smaller, less contested market, and it's worth applying it deliberately rather than trusting whichever app has the most downloads.

## Why does post-quantum-ready encryption matter for Indian users specifically? [#](#why-does-post-quantum-ready-encryption-matter-for-indian-users-specifically)

**Quick Answer**

Post-quantum-ready encryption protects the VPN handshake against future quantum decryption of traffic recorded today  -  a threat that applies to anyone whose data needs to stay private for years, including financial transactions, government service logins, and private communications that many Indian users route through mobile apps daily.

This is worth grounding in specifics rather than treating as a generic buzzword.

A huge share of daily digital life in India now runs through a phone  -  UPI payments, banking apps, Aadhaar-linked services, government portals, and messaging apps carrying years of private conversations. Much of that data needs to stay confidential for a long time, not just today.

That's exactly the window relevant to the "harvest now, decrypt later" threat: an adversary can record your encrypted VPN traffic today without being able to read it, store that recording, and attempt decryption once a sufficiently powerful quantum computer exists  -  mainstream estimates place that somewhere between the early 2030s and mid-2040s. The vulnerable part of a VPN connection isn't the bulk encryption of your traffic, which AES-256 handles and which holds up reasonably well even against quantum attacks  -  it's the handshake, the initial step where your device and the VPN server agree on a shared secret using public-key cryptography that classical elliptic-curve math currently protects but that a capable quantum computer is expected to eventually break.

OllaVPN addresses this with a hybrid handshake combining a classical, well-established algorithm with a newer, quantum-resistant one based on NIST's standardized ML-KEM algorithm. Because both run side by side and combine into the final secret, an attacker would need to break both, not just one, to compromise your connection. This costs a few extra milliseconds during connection setup  -  not a noticeable amount of speed or battery  -  while meaningfully extending how long your data stays protected.

Given how much of daily financial and personal life in India already runs through a phone, this isn't a hypothetical concern reserved for governments or large companies  -  it applies to anyone whose UPI history, banking logins, or private messages should still be private a decade from now.
